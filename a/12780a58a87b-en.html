Tiny Flying Robot Survives Swats and Crushes Like an Insect

California researchers build a 185 mg flying robot that withstands extreme impacts using flexible polymer artificial muscles.

Inspired by the resilience of flying insects, a prototype micro-robot developed by researchers from the University of California (UCLA and UC Davis) pushes the limits of durability for miniature drones. Weighing just 185 mg—less than one-fifth of a gram—it generates enough lift to support three times its own weight.

Unlike conventional flying robots, its structure relies entirely on flexible polymers, eliminating rigid mechanical components. Its propulsion system uses an artificial muscle made of a relaxor ferroelectric polymer film, which deforms under an electric field (electrostriction). This deformation directly drives a pair of flexible, cone-shaped wings that can bend without permanent creasing during impacts. The robot can survive violent collisions, such as a swat from a flyswatter or compression 11,000 times its weight.

The asymmetric, deformable wings optimize lift by generating upward thrust with each beat (30 Hz). In tests, the prototype reached an average climb speed of 40 cm/s, with a lift-to-weight ratio of 3.0 and a lift-to-power ratio of 8.3 mN/W. However, it remains dependent on external power via wires and lacks autonomous navigation systems.

Researchers plan to integrate batteries, sensors, and control algorithms for independent flight. Eventually, such robots could operate in confined spaces for search-and-rescue missions, hazardous environment inspections, or artificial pollination.
