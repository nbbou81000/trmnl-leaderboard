Ultra-Thin MoS₂ Transistors Could Extend Moore’s Law

Researchers leverage molybdenum disulfide to build 5 nm transistors outperforming silicon’s theoretical limits.

The semiconductor industry faces a roadblock as silicon transistors shrink below 5 nanometers, where leakage currents degrade efficiency and increase power consumption. Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University have developed an alternative using molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), a two-dimensional semiconductor just one atom thick.

MoS₂ mitigates quantum tunneling thanks to its high effective mass and 1.8 eV bandgap. The team optimized the transistor design by introducing an air gap beneath the channel to minimize parasitic coupling between source, drain, and channel. This architecture enhances gate control over current flow, a critical challenge at nanoscale dimensions. Another key innovation involves overlapping the gate with the MoS₂ contact regions, leveraging the material’s low density of states to better regulate carrier density.

Tested devices achieved a subthreshold swing of 88 mV/decade at a physical channel length of 4.8 nanometers, with an on/off current ratio exceeding one million. Fabricated in arrays on 4-inch wafers using photolithography, 138 transistors showed reproducible results with a median swing of 92 mV/decade. While challenges remain—such as a negative threshold voltage and room for improvement in on-state performance—the work demonstrates that material innovation could extend Moore’s Law beyond silicon’s limits.
