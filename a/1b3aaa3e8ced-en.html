Infrared: From Remote Controls to Data Protocols

Infrared, often overlooked, provides directional, secure, and energy-efficient communication far beyond simple remote controls.

Infrared (IR) protocols are among the oldest and most widespread wireless communication technologies, despite their understated role. Their main advantage lies in their directionality: the signal does not pass through obstacles, ensuring better privacy than technologies like Bluetooth, which is vulnerable to interference and passive eavesdropping. IR remote controls, which replaced wired and ultrasonic models, rely on protocols such as NEC, Sony SIRC, or Philips RC-5, using a carrier wave around 38 kHz and specific encoding methods (e.g., *pulse distance encoding* for NEC or *Manchester encoding* for RC-5).

These protocols, simple and easy to implement, are now ubiquitous in consumer devices, from RGB LED strips to industrial systems. Open-source projects, like an implementation on STM32F0 to control 24-key remotes, or application notes from Texas Instruments for the MSP430, highlight their accessibility for hobbyists. Some smartphones, particularly from Chinese brands, still include an IR blaster, turning the device into a universal remote, though this feature has become rare.

Beyond remotes: IrDA and its derivatives

IrDA (*Infrared Data Association*) extends these principles for higher-speed data transfers, up to several megabits per second. Unlike remote controls, IrDA uses advanced modulation and encoding techniques, such as IrSimple, optimized to transfer a 500 KB image in under a second between compatible devices. Communications typically operate in half-duplex to avoid interference, with a maximum range of two meters, or as low as 20 cm for low-power versions.

These protocols are widely used in smart meters (electricity, gas, heat), where low power consumption and the security provided by line-of-sight are critical. While radio-based alternatives (such as DLMS/COSEM) are gaining traction, IR remains preferred for its simplicity and lack of interference with surrounding networks. Finally, consoles like the Nintendo 3DS leveraged IrDA-SIR for peripherals like the Circle Pad Pro or Amiibo reader, proving that this technology remains relevant even in modern applications.
