Conductive Concrete: 3D-Printed Supercapacitors Embedded in Cement

Researchers embed cement-based supercapacitors into 3D-printed structures, merging energy storage with mechanical strength.

A research team has developed a 3D-printed cement-based supercapacitor that combines energy storage with structural strength. This innovative material could enable buildings to store electricity while serving their load-bearing function.

The main challenge was balancing thickness and conductivity. Traditional supercapacitors suffer from a fragile interface between electrodes and separators, while thicker devices increase the distance ions must travel. To overcome these issues, the researchers created a conductive ink from cement, carbon nanotubes, and carbon black. This ink is deposited via direct ink writing—a 3D printing technique using extrusion—into a cement-based separator in an interdigitated pattern. This design, where positive and negative electrodes interlock like fingers without touching, reduces ion travel distance and maximizes the surface area for electrolyte interaction.

The resulting structure is monolithic, with no distinct electrode-separator interface, improving mechanical resistance. The supercapacitor achieves a surface capacitance of 162.14 mF/cm², an energy density of 22.52 μWh/cm² at 1.0 V, and a power density of 0.49 mW/cm². Its compressive strength reaches 23 MPa, comparable to some conventional structural concretes.

To validate the concept, the researchers printed three supercapacitors on a single slab, connected them, and powered an LED array with the stored energy. However, limitations remain: performance drops sharply around 0 °F, likely due to electrolyte freezing and moisture changes. Despite this, the technology paves the way for buildings that could store locally generated solar or wind energy, or smaller versions to power embedded sensors or emergency lighting.
