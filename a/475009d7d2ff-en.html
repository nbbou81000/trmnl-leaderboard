OpenBDX: An Open-Source Bipedal Robot Inspired by Disney

A robotics engineer recreates a Disney bipedal robot as an open-source project, backed by a growing community.

Jaime Machuca, a robotics and autonomous vehicles engineer, launched OpenBDX, an open-source project to recreate and enhance Disney Research’s bipedal BDX droids. These robots, showcased at Nvidia GTC in March 2024, stand out for their realism and ability to elicit immediate human reactions, as if they were characters in their own right.

The project builds on Disney Research’s work, which provided foundational insights into mechanical design, actuation, and control strategies while leaving technical challenges unresolved. Machuca initially focused on mechanics, using Shapr3D to design legs around the actuators identified in Disney’s paper. The project quickly expanded beyond a solo effort, especially after meeting Mimir Reynisson at DroidCon in June 2024—a fellow builder already advancing his own version.

The team grew further with Florian Bieregger, a mechanical engineer tasked with redesigning the leg components to improve durability. OpenBDX is mechanically complex: each leg features five degrees of freedom (hip, knee, ankle), and the torso adds four more for the neck and head, plus two servos for the antennas. Primary actuators come from Unitree (A1 and Go-M8010-6), while the head uses Dynamixel servos. Two Nvidia Jetson Orin NX modules handle computing, one in the torso and one in the head, connected via Ethernet.

The system’s core is PuddleDuck, a custom board designed by Mimir Reynisson that bridges the Jetson modules and motors via a Teensy microcontroller. It also manages audio, NeoPixel LEDs, IMU sensors, and R/C inputs. Movement feedback comes from depth cameras, a BNO085 IMU, and the actuators themselves. The robot learns to walk using control policies trained in simulation with tools like MJLab and PPO, then transferred to the physical hardware.

The biggest challenge wasn’t making the droid look realistic while stationary—it was enabling it to move stably and expressively like a character. Unlike directly piloted astromechs, OpenBDX must coordinate 14 joints in real time to maintain balance, a task impossible to perform manually. The project offers tools like OpenBDX Studio, Duckmation, and GaitForge to simplify movement creation and animation.
