Robots Learn Teamwork Without Communication

Japanese researchers demonstrate how multiple robots can learn coordination by observing a single demonstrator, without communication or GPS.

Coordinating dozens of autonomous robots is far more complex than operating a single machine. Interference between robots creates traffic jams and reduces overall efficiency, a problem difficult to solve without massive training data. A team of Japanese researchers from the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST), Institute of Science Tokyo, and the University of Tokyo has proposed an innovative approach: teaching robots to collaborate by observing a single demonstrator.

Their method relies on imitation learning, where each robot observes and replicates the actions of a robot manually controlled via a joystick. No inter-robot communication or external positioning system is required. To validate this approach, the scientists used mobile robots based on the Raspberry Pi Mouse V3 platform, equipped with a Raspberry Pi 4 and a wide-angle camera (120°). Their task was to gather 28 orange blocks in a 3.5 m² space by pushing them toward a central area.

The robots were trained using two sets of demonstrations: one with stationary robots in the environment, the other with robots moving autonomously. The control system uses a lightweight neural network combining MobileNetV2 for visual processing and a mixture-of-experts architecture. Two LSTM-based expert networks analyze image sequences, while a routing network determines their contribution to movement commands. With just 2.4 million parameters, the model runs entirely on the Raspberry Pi 4s without GPU acceleration, processing camera observations and generating velocity commands at 5 Hz.

Tests were conducted with groups of up to 40 robots. Policies trained with demonstrations involving stationary robots performed better than those using moving robots, likely due to the increased complexity introduced by the latter. Increasing the number of robots did not proportionally improve productivity, primarily because congestion became more problematic in the workspace. The researchers highlight that this method enables robots to acquire useful coordination behaviors, but anticipating each other’s movements remains a challenge for efficient scaling.
