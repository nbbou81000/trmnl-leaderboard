Commodore’s VICModem: The First Sub-$100 Phone Modem in 1982

In 1982, Commodore made waves in the tech world by releasing the VICModem, a telephone modem priced under $100—a groundbreaking feat at the time. While 300 baud modems typically cost twice as much and 1200 baud units exceeded $600, Commodore’s offering democratized access to digital communication for home users.

Unlike its competitors, the VICModem wasn’t designed in-house. Instead, Commodore outsourced its development to Anchor Automation, a well-known modem manufacturer. The device relied on a Motorola MC14412 chip, theoretically capable of 600 baud, though the modem was officially rated for 300 baud. To cut costs, Commodore opted for a direct phone line connection, bypassing the bulky acoustic coupler (a device with a microphone and speaker that clamped onto a handset). However, this required users to manually dial the number before plugging the handset into the modem.

A logistical hurdle arose during its Canadian launch. Commodore had planned to bundle phones with the modem, but the selected models featured hardwired handsets, making them incompatible with the required connection method. Worse, these phones would have violated Canadian regulations, which banned modular jacks for handsets at the time. To resolve the issue, Commodore included the VIC-1605, a Y-adapter cable allowing both the phone and modem to share a single wall outlet.

Despite these cost-cutting measures, the VICModem stands as a testament to 1980s ingenuity, proving that functional technology could be made affordable. Today, locating such an antique modem is a challenge, but its legacy highlights how rudimentary systems laid the groundwork for the digital age.
