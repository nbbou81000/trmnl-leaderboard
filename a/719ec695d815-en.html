The Altair 8800: The First Affordable Personal Computer in 1975

In 1975, the Altair 8800 marked the entry of personal computers into households, despite prohibitive costs and major technical limitations.

In December 1974, the January 1975 issue of Popular Electronics introduced the Altair 8800, a computer sold as a kit or pre-assembled, making personal computing accessible for the first time. Before its launch, owning a computer was nearly impossible for the average person, requiring rare components or exorbitant sums for surplus minicomputers. Developed by MITS, the Altair used an Intel 8080 processor running at 2 MHz, though its real-world speed was much lower due to multi-cycle instructions.

The base unit, consisting of switches and LEDs, needed expansions to become usable. A 4 KB dynamic RAM board cost $338, while a 1 KB static RAM board was $134 (or $209 fully loaded). Program storage relied on cassette tape readers (priced at $174), as the system lacked built-in memory. Dedicated terminals like the Comter 256, with its 32-character display, added to the cost: $885 for the base model, or $1,500 for a Teletype ASR 33, the only viable alternative despite its sluggish 10-character-per-second output.

The total cost of a functional system exceeded $3,000—equivalent to nearly $19,000 today. At the time, this was a staggering amount: more than a new car and nearly a third of the median annual U.S. income. Yet, the Altair 8800 represented a pivotal moment, proving that personal computers could exist and paving the way for the modern computing era.
