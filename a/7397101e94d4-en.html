Philips AV7300: Converting Analog RF TV Signal to Composite Video

An 80s Philips box converts retro computer RF signals to composite video, without a screen or digital electronics.

Computers from the 1970s and 1980s, like the ZX81, transmitted their video signal via an RF (radio frequency) cable to be displayed on TVs of the time. With the end of analog broadcasts in 2012, these signals are no longer compatible with modern screens, and filming a CRT (cathode ray tube) screen introduces distortions like rolling black bars.

The Philips AV7300, a device produced in 1987, offers a simple solution: it captures the RF signal via an antenna and converts it into composite video and audio output on RCA (phono) connectors. Unlike a standard TV, it has no built-in screen, making it a dedicated tool for signal recovery. Inside, its analog circuit is based on the IF (intermediate frequency) section of the Philips System 4 (K40) chassis, with no digital components.

The device can store twelve presets, each manually configured using a rotary knob and an LED indicator. After replacing degraded rubber feet and checking capacitor condition, the user found that the box only outputs video once it locks onto the RF signal. Tests confirmed its operation with a BBC Micro and an Amiga 1200.

Buyers should pay attention to video standard compatibility: PAL I, PAL B/G, or NTSC. A mismatch can result in a picture without sound. More details are available on andrewdorey.com.
