Restoring a 1984 Omnibot with AI and Raspberry Pi 5

A 1984 toy robot is revived with a Raspberry Pi 5, an AI camera, and 3D printing, without modifying its original electronics.

In 1984, Tomy released the Omnibot, a toy robot capable of moving, playing cassette tapes, and carrying drinks on a built-in tray. Despite its futuristic design, its capabilities were limited to remote control and pre-recorded commands. Four decades later, maker Mario Cruz breathed new life into one of these robots by integrating a Raspberry Pi 5, a Sony IMX500 AI camera, and a 3D-printed companion called Ring-O.

The project started after a damaged Omnibot, corroded by old batteries, was donated to Moonlighter FabLab in Miami. After mechanical and electronic restoration, Cruz leveraged the robot’s original audio-based control system, which used tone frequencies to trigger actions like moving forward or turning. By adding a Bluetooth cassette adapter and a Python script, he could control the Omnibot without replacing its original electronics.

Ring-O, the 3D-printed companion, houses a Raspberry Pi 5 (8 GB RAM), the AI camera, and a 128×128-pixel OLED display acting as an animated eye. The camera runs a YOLO11-nano model to detect 80 object classes (people, animals, etc.) directly on its hardware, offloading processing from the Raspberry Pi. Initially, Cruz used a cloud-hosted large language model to guide the robot, but slow response times (15 seconds) and poor performance led him to implement hardcoded movement rules for instant reactivity.

Ring-O reacts to detected objects: its eye blinks, follows movement, and animates. A web interface allows sending commands like "find a person" or "locate a shoe." A language model also generates spoken descriptions of what the camera sees. The project’s source code is available under an MIT license on GitHub.

The most impressive aspect is that the robot’s original motor control electronics, over 40 years old, still function perfectly.
