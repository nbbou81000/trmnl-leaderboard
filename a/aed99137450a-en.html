Home Assistant: 4 Limitations and Their Workarounds

Home Assistant automates almost everything, but four functions remain out of its reach without external solutions.

Home Assistant excels at automating smart devices, but certain scenarios still fall outside its direct control. For instance, it cannot detect its own server failure because it is inactive when down. To address this, tools like Uptime Kuma continuously monitor hosted services and send alerts (via Telegram, for example) as soon as a malfunction occurs. This solution can be deployed on a separate device, such as a Raspberry Pi, or in a dedicated container on the same machine, though the latter option carries increased risk in case of a system-wide outage.

Another limitation is Home Assistant’s reliance on electrical power. During a power outage, the server shuts down, risking even data corruption. Two solutions exist: using a device with a built-in battery (such as a laptop) or, more reliably, an uninterruptible power supply (UPS). The UPS keeps the system running during brief outages and can even trigger a safe shutdown of devices when the battery runs low.

Remote access is also compromised without an internet connection. Traditional methods (Home Assistant Cloud, Tailscale, Cloudflare Tunnel) require an external link. An innovative alternative uses LoRa, a long-range radio technology. One user connected a LoRa radio to their home server and another to a portable device. A Python script relays commands between the two, allowing control of lights or checking system status, even offline. Range remains limited by distance but covers reasonable areas.

Finally, Home Assistant cannot unlock a door if the smart lock’s battery dies. A backup solution (physical key or emergency power) is essential to avoid being locked out.
