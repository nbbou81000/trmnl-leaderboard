A Single-Motor Drone Using Variable-Pitch Propeller

An engineer builds a drone that flies with just one motor, no servos or swashplate, by dynamically adjusting blade pitch.

Most drones rely on four or more motors to stabilize and steer, using independent speed adjustments for each rotor. Joshua Bird challenged this norm by building a drone that flies with a single motor, dispensing with servos and swashplates. His solution hinges on a custom propeller hub where blades, mounted on 45-degree hinges, adjust their pitch dynamically in response to rapid motor speed changes.

During acceleration or deceleration, blade inertia causes them to pivot briefly: one blade rises while the other dips, creating an imbalance in lift that tilts the drone. By timing these adjustments to specific points in each rotation, Bird steers the aircraft using only the motor’s speed variations. After testing a dual-hinge propeller assembly, he refined the design into a compact hub with a central pivot, 3D-printed in carbon fiber-reinforced polyphthalamide to endure the forces involved. Counterweights and miniature bearings ensure smooth blade movement under load.

Controlling the motor required a specialized electronic speed controller (ESC). Standard ESCs, which rely on back electromotive force, lose synchronization during rapid speed fluctuations. Bird replaced it with a Moteus R4 servo drive, using a magnetic encoder to track rotor position in real time. The six-inch propeller, also printed in carbon fiber-reinforced polyphthalamide, reaches nearly 20,000 RPM with acceleration and deceleration rates up to 300 times per second.

Excessive vibrations at these speeds necessitated stiffer blades. To counteract the motor’s torque, Bird incorporated fixed anti-torque vanes into the 3D-printed TPU body, reducing but not eliminating body rotation. An infrared beacon system was developed to periodically correct the drone’s heading estimate, disrupted by magnetic interference from the motor.

The prototype achieved stable indoor flight, including controlled hovering and directional movement, demonstrating that a single motor can provide both lift and directional control—albeit with added complexity.
