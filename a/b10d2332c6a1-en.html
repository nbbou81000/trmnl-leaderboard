LEO, the First Business Computer, Turns 75

LEO, the first purpose-built electronic business computer, marks its 75th anniversary with an event hosted by the Centre for Computing History.

In 1951, British food giant J. Lyons and Company launched LEO (Lyons Electronic Office), the first computer designed specifically for business use. Developed in collaboration with Cambridge University, LEO was based on the EDSAC, a research computer from the 1940s. Unlike military or academic machines of the time, LEO was built to automate the logistics of a large corporation, handling production and distribution for food products like cakes, biscuits, and tea.

The project began after Lyons executives discovered the ENIAC in the U.S., a groundbreaking but impractical machine for their needs. They turned to the EDSAC, which was more suitable, and adapted its components to create a commercial system. LEO relied on vacuum tubes and filled an entire room, yet it proved that computers could serve real-world business applications beyond labs or battlefields. The LEO series evolved through the 1970s, with the last model still in use by the British Post Office.

To celebrate its 75th anniversary, the Centre for Computing History is hosting a dedicated event. While no original LEO survives in museums today, the National Museum of Computing at Bletchley Park features a recreation of the EDSAC, the machine that inspired LEO.
