Radon: Understanding Risks and Detection Solutions

A colorless, odorless gas from uranium decay, radon builds up in homes and poses a lung cancer risk.

Radon is a radioactive gas produced by the decay of uranium in soil. Colorless and odorless, it is heavier than air and seeps into buildings through cracks, joints, or pipes, especially when indoor pressure is lower than soil pressure. Trapped indoors, it decays by emitting alpha particles, damaging lung cells. According to the EPA, it is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States, after smoking.

Detecting radon: methods and tools

The simplest detection kits use activated charcoal, which absorbs radon over a few days before being analyzed in a lab. These low-cost tests (under $50) are placed on the ground floor or basement, in a stable, draft-free location. For long-term assessment, plastic film detectors are used: alpha particles leave microscopic tracks, counted after chemical processing. Electronic monitors, more precise, measure concentration variations continuously, influenced by weather, season, or ventilation.

Radon is measured in picocuries per liter (pCi/L). The EPA recommends mitigation starting at 4 pCi/L, though no concentration is considered entirely safe. Tests should be repeated every two years to verify the effectiveness of measures.

Reducing exposure: solutions and limits

To limit accumulation, forced ventilation of the soil beneath the concrete slab is often necessary. A depressurization system, consisting of a fan and PVC pipes, sucks the gas out and vents it outside, above the roofline. While installable by an experienced DIYer, it requires drilling the slab and ensuring uniform depression. The average cost of such an installation is around $1,000. Some local regulations require professional certification for the system.
