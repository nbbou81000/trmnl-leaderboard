A Brain-Inspired Camera Sees Through Dense Fog

Brown University researchers develop an imaging system that filters light interference to detect moving objects in fog or murky water.

Conventional cameras often fail in low-visibility conditions like fog or murky water, as light scatters before reaching the sensor. Researchers at Brown University have developed an imaging system inspired by the human brain to overcome this issue. Instead of capturing full images continuously, their solution uses a dynamic vision sensor (DVS), a type of camera where each pixel reacts only to sudden brightness changes. This approach filters out static backgrounds, such as slowly evolving fog, while detecting fast-moving objects.

The raw data generated by the DVS, in the form of asynchronous event streams, is then processed by a deep spiking neural network (SNN) designed to mimic the visual cortex. The network splits into two branches: one locates the object, the other reconstructs its silhouette. Using leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, the system accumulates information over time, improving the detection of moving objects rather than relying on a single snapshot.

Tests conducted by the researchers validated the system’s effectiveness across various scenarios. Handwritten digits hidden behind a scattering material were reconstructed with a structural similarity score of 0.9577. Bird silhouettes moving in a tank of turbid water achieved an average score of 0.9593. The DVS consumes about 28 milliwatts, and the neural network requires 18 times less energy than a conventional artificial neural network. Sequences of 18 steps are processed in under 15 milliseconds.

Despite these promising results, the system still has limitations. It only detects moving or optically changing objects, so very slow-moving targets may go unnoticed. Additionally, it reconstructs silhouettes rather than detailed images, and low lighting reduces the DVS’s sensitivity. The researchers are now exploring solutions like image intensifiers, time-of-flight techniques, and binocular event cameras to address these challenges. In the future, this technology could equip autonomous vehicles, search-and-rescue drones, or underwater robots for better navigation in difficult conditions.
