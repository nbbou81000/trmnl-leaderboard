Embedded 3D Printing with Liquids on a Bricked Prusa FDM Printer

A maker repurposes a broken Prusa i3 FDM printer into an embedded 3D printing machine for liquids, exploring new possibilities with E3DP.

A maker known as Riley salvaged a broken Prusa i3 FDM printer with a blown-out hot-end and repurposed it into an embedded 3D printing machine for liquids. This technique, called Embedded 3D Printing (E3DP), allows printing structures impossible with standard filament extrusion by extruding liquid into a supportive medium.

E3DP works by depositing liquid materials—such as cream cheese or hair gel—into a supportive bath, enabling the creation of complex shapes. Unlike traditional FDM, where plastic hardens in open air, E3DP supports viscous fluids or biological materials. Riley replaced the original hot-end with an open-source Allstruder syringe pump and a Caribou Duet 3 motherboard to control extrusion accurately.

His experiments highlighted major challenges: while cream cheese worked well for testing, two-part epoxy proved difficult due to its short 40-minute curing window. A trial with Sylgard 184 silicone also failed, as hair gel additives inhibited proper curing. Riley documented the process in a detailed video, emphasizing the current limitations of supportive materials and potential research paths to improve print quality.
