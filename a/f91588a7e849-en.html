Lycurgan Cup’s Dichroic Effect Recreated Using Gold and Silver Nanoparticles

Researchers recreate the dichroic effect of the Lycurgus Cup, a 3rd or 4th century Roman artifact, using gold and silver nanoparticles embedded in modern glass.

The Lycurgan Cup, a 3rd or 4th century Roman glass artifact, is renowned for its dichroic effect: it appears green under front lighting and red when light passes through from behind. This phenomenon, long unexplained, stems from gold and silver nanoparticles embedded in its structure.

Researchers at the University of Sheffield have replicated this effect by embedding similar nanoparticles into modern glass. Unlike previous attempts, their method extends beyond glass objects—they applied it to a modified Atlas statue, creating an artwork with comparable optical properties. This breakthrough relies on precise measurement and dosing of nanoparticles, made possible by contemporary technologies.

The dichroic effect is not tied to lighting conditions alone but depends on the observer’s position relative to the light source. This distinction was highlighted in comments, emphasizing that perceived color varies with the direction of light passing through the material.
