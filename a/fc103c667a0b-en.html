Researchers Use Thermal Noise to Transmit Data Without Radio Waves

Stanford researchers turn natural electrical noise into an ultra-low-power data transmission method.

Traditional wireless communication relies on actively generating radio waves to transmit data, a process that consumes significant energy—an issue for battery-powered devices. Researchers at Stanford University have demonstrated a groundbreaking alternative: using thermal noise, the random electrical static produced by electron motion in conductive materials, to send information without emitting radio frequencies.

Their method, called thermal noise communication, manipulates this natural phenomenon. The transmitter switches between two electrical states (open or closed circuit), altering how the antenna absorbs or reflects ambient thermal noise. Each state encodes a binary bit (0 or 1). The receiver, equipped with an amplifier acting as an "electron refrigerator," amplifies these tiny variations to distinguish them. Unlike conventional methods, neither the transmitter nor the receiver actually heats up or cools down—the perceived signal changes instead.

Experiments conducted in an insulated chamber to block external interference achieved data rates in the kilobits-per-second range. By developing a mathematical model, the team optimized their system, increasing transmission speeds by 100 times. While these speeds are far below those of traditional radio technologies, the energy advantage is substantial: a thermal noise transmitter could use up to 1,000 times less power than a conventional radio-frequency system.

This innovation holds promise for low-power sensor networks, RFID systems, or environments sensitive to interference (medical equipment, radio telescopes). Future work includes testing multiple simultaneous transmitters and reliability in real-world conditions outside controlled environments.
